| A16 | Ratio definitions and their basis (net vs gross). LR and ER are on a NET basis, so ER (gross expenses over net premium) may be inflated for low-retention insurers (supervisor comment). Definitions as printed, by report (line numbers in `data/raw/nic_text/`): **Claims ratio** = net claims incurred / (net earned premium + reinsurance commission income) in 2019 (lines 4009-4011), 2020 (4993-4994) and 2022 (3799-3800); 2024 (IFRS 17, 3406-3408): incurred claims and adjustments to the liability for incurred claims / Net Insurance Revenue (incl. reinsurance commission income). **Total expense ratio** = (management + commission expense) / net earned premium in 2019 (4070-4074); / (net earned premium + reinsurance income) in 2020 (5046-5050) and 2022 (3852-3855); 2024 (3449-3453): (attributable + non-attributable expenses) / Insurance Revenue, which is GROSS of reinsurance under IFRS 17. **Combined ratio** = claims ratio + expense ratio in all reports (2019 4125-4127; 2020 5133-5135; 2022 3903-3905; 2024 3498-3500) | Overlapping years are identical across reports (A3) despite the 2019 -> 2020 change in the ER denominator, so either the 2019 wording was imprecise or history was carried forward without recomputation; the texts cannot settle which. In 2023-24, ER is gross (Insurance Revenue) while LR is net (Net Insurance Revenue), so CR mixes bases: a further reason 2023-24 are not pooled with 2016-22. Caution: the printed 2023-24 ER does not behave like a gross ratio. For the 25 insurers present 2021-24, insurer-level ER barely moves from 2022 to 2023 (median change -1 pp; medians 82 -> 76) although the stated denominator moves from net premium to gross Insurance Revenue (median retention 73%, so a true switch should cut ER by about a quarter). Either the 2024 wording is imprecise or the IFRS 17 expense numerator is wider; the texts cannot settle which. Treating it as gross in `industry_trends_gross.csv` makes ER_g jump from 42 (2022) to 70 (2023), so the 2023-24 rows of that series are not comparable with 2015-22. Step 11 (`cr_mismatch_analysis.csv`) re-tests the 2015-16 CR mismatch (A6): with the `cr_nic_mismatch` flag (\|d\| > 1) there are 21 of 23 comparable rows in 2015 and 20 of 23 in 2016 (median \|d\| 12.0 and 10.5), plus 2 in 2021; an earlier off-pipeline check found 19 and 18 of 21, on a sample that could not be reconstructed. No retention adjustment reproduces the printed CR (all six candidate formulas have negative R-squared); corr(d, RET) = -0.09 but corr(d, ER) = -0.93; in 3 rows the printed CR is below ER alone (IMPERIAL 2015, NSIA 2015: ER 495, CR 140; UNIQUE 2016), impossible under any additive definition. So the 2015-16 CR is a tabulation problem, not a net/gross basis difference | Gross-basis ratios `LR_g`, `ER_g`, `CR_g` = LR, ER x RET/100 (2015-22 only; missing for 2023-24, whose ER is already gross). Approximation: assumes NEP ~ NWP and gross earned premium ~ GWP and ignores reinsurance commission income (RIC) in NIC's denominator, so the gross-basis figures are UNDERSTATED by the factor NEP/(NEP+RIC) (direction certain, size unknown: RIC is not published per insurer). `CR_g` omits the net cost of reinsurance and is NOT a profitability measure; net CR remains the profitability measure and `ER_g` measures operating efficiency only. Step 04 re-estimates M1, M3, M5 on the gross basis, with and without RET (`robust_gross_basis.csv`), and gives the GWP- and NWP-weighted gross industry series (`industry_trends_gross.csv`) and the ER-retention correlations (`er_ret_correlation.csv`) | State the definitions and the gross-basis robustness in the methodology chapter |
